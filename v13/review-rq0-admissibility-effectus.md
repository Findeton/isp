## THE K1 ADJUDICATION, WITH THE STAGE-5 ANSWER

### K1(a) — rigidity re-proved

**Theorem 3.1 is correct, and its proof is correct as written.** Re-proved independently:

Let $\pi=A(B)$ and suppose (i) holds. (i-b) gives $\mathfrak F=\operatorname{Pres}_L(\pi)$. Since $\operatorname{comp}(\mathrm{id})$ is discrete and discrete refines every partition, $\mathrm{id}\in\mathfrak F$. Since $\operatorname{sup}_{\mathrm{id}}(j)=\{j\}$, the identity separates every distinct pair, so $\ker(\mathfrak F)$ is discrete; (i-a) then forces $\pi$ discrete. Conversely $\operatorname{Pres}_L(\text{discrete})=\{F:\operatorname{comp}(F)=\text{discrete}\}$ contains $\mathrm{id}$, whose kernel is discrete. $\square$

I also confirmed the equivalence the proof rests on — Cycle B's Definition 2.3 criterion ("images of distinct blocks pairwise disjoint") and Lemma 3.2's ("$\operatorname{comp}(F)$ refines $\pi$") — by implementing both routes separately and cross-checking them; they agree everywhere I ran them.

**A sharper statement is available and is the one the paper should carry.** Condition (i) holds at $\pi$ **iff** $\operatorname{Obs}(L,\pi)=\varnothing$ (for $\mathfrak F\neq\varnothing$). Proof: under (i-b), $\ker(\mathfrak F)\le\operatorname{comp}(F)\le\pi$ for any $F\in\mathfrak F$, so (i-a) is equivalent to "no member of $\operatorname{Pres}_L(\pi)$ separates a pair inside a block", which is exactly $\operatorname{Obs}=\varnothing$. This unifies Section 3 and Section 8 in one line — rigidity is the statement that $\operatorname{Obs}$ is never empty, and the forging cost is the distance to condition (i) — and it makes the identity's role visible as *the always-available obstruction member* rather than as a special hypothesis.

### K1(b) — the identity-free control: genuine law, but structurally forced

**Closure: verified, by hand and by machine.** $a=(0,0,2)$, $b=(0,2,2)$ on $\{0,1,2\}$; the full table is $a\circ a=a$, $b\circ b=b$, $a\circ b=b$, $b\circ a=a$ — closed under either composition convention, both supports left-total. Under Definition 2.1 it is a genuine admitted law. **It is not, and cannot be, a law of the committed class:** the scope box declares DET, FUNNEL, REV, ALL and the counter-law, and §8 states that each contains the identity by construction. The paper labels the control correctly ("delete the identity from the predecessor's committed law") and does not smuggle it into the class.

Is the escape *bespoke*? **No — it is forced, and that is a stronger statement than the paper makes.** Census over every composition-closed law on three configurations generated by at most three elements (687 laws):

- laws admitting at least one **proper** (non-discrete) boundary: **428**;
- of those 428, laws containing the identity: **0**; laws containing a permutation: **0**;
- laws containing the identity: **259**; containing a permutation: **259**; containing a permutation but not the identity: **0**;
- identity-containing laws with a proper boundary: **0**; identity-free laws with **no** proper boundary: **0**.

So at $n=3$ the paper's §3.4 sentence is exactly right — "it selects them exactly when the law admits no operation that leaves the configurations where they are" — and it is a **biconditional the paper asserts but does not prove**. Only the forward half (rigidity) is proved. A 1018-law sample at $n=4$ gives 0 counterexamples in both directions.

Two structural facts the paper should carry with it:

1. **At a finite carrier, a composition-closed law contains a permutation iff it contains the identity** (any permutation has finite order, so $\sigma^k=\mathrm{id}$ lands in the law; measured: 259 = 259, zero perm-but-no-id). Equivalently, since pairwise-disjoint left-total supports on a finite set force singletons and injectivity, "contains the identity" = "contains any reversible operation". **"Laws that can idle" and "laws with any reversibility" are the same class.** This is what rigidity is really about, and it is a much bigger claim than idling.
2. **Under the control, the carrier's own algebra is *inadmissible*.** $\operatorname{Pres}_{\{a,b\}}(\text{discrete})=\varnothing$, so $\ker(\varnothing)$ is the one-atom boundary and (i-a) fails at the discrete boundary. The control's admissible set is exactly $\{\{01\mid2\},\{0\mid12\}\}$ — the two proper boundaries and *not* the discrete one. The paper reports the positive half and omits this, which is the sharper half: the axiom is not "always at least the carrier".

### K1(c) — THE STAGE-5 CONSEQUENCE QUESTION, ANSWERED

**Are coarse patches ever admissible under physical laws?** **No.** Not under any law of the committed class, and not under any law containing a single reversible operation — which, by the fact above, is the same condition. Unitary dynamics, any law closed under a symmetry group, any law containing the zero-time channel, and every one of DET/FUNNEL/REV/ALL/counter-law are all rigid. The class where proper charts survive is exactly the strictly non-idling class: laws in which *no* admitted operation leaves the configurations where they are. That class is not empty (428 of 687 at $n=3$), but it excludes reversibility altogether, and an atlas whose charts are proper coarse-grainings cannot be built over any law the corpus has committed to.

**Does the axiom therefore need a relative or approximate form?** **Yes, for any atlas of proper charts. But the obvious relativization is refuted, and I refute it here rather than recommending it.**

The natural repair is to relativize (i-b) — the clause that drags in the identity — from the whole admitted law $L$ to a declared composition-closed sub-law $L_P\subseteq L$ that the patch actually runs. **Measured: this hands the forgery straight back.** Take $f=(0,0,2,3,4)$ and $L_P=\langle f\rangle=\{f\}$ (idempotent, closed, identity-free). Under $L_P$ the forged member one — atoms $\{0,1\},\{2\},\{3\},\{4\}$ — passes **all four clauses** and is ADMISSIBLE; member two becomes inadmissible; and the refinement statistic that §7.1 uses to separate the forgery collapses from $1/16$ to $0$, so even the Feynman separator dies. A declared sub-law is adversary data exactly as a declared carrier is; relativizing there moves the block from the carrier to the sub-law and loses the cycle's central discriminator in the move.

**The viable direction is the approximate form, and the paper has already computed its separator.** The quantity that survives the relativization test is the one the adversary does not control once the state and the instrument are committed: the refinement statistic $\delta$, exactly $0$ at the admissible patch and exactly $1/16$ at the forged one. An $\varepsilon$-form — a boundary's asserted identification is admissible when no admitted task resolves inside it *above the committed tester's resolution* — keeps the forged patch out at $\delta=1/16$, degrades gracefully as the law becomes rich, and does not require the law to be non-reversible. This is offered as adjudication input; it is a direction, not a result, and nothing in this cycle establishes it.

## THE ENTAILMENT PROOF AUDIT (K4)

**Theorem 3.2 is correct.** Re-proved: by (i-a), any $j,j'$ in one block have $\operatorname{sup}_F(j)=\operatorname{sup}_F(j')$, which is non-empty by left-totality, so $j$ and $j'$ collide and $A(B)$ refines $\operatorname{comp}(F)$; by (i-b), $\operatorname{comp}(F)$ refines $A(B)$; equality follows. $\square$ Only the $A(B)\le\ker(\mathfrak F)$ half of (i-a) is used.

Three audit findings:

- **The proof silently uses left-totality.** "Those configurations therefore share a later configuration" is false if $\operatorname{sup}_F(j)=\varnothing$ — two configurations with equal empty supports do not collide, and $\operatorname{comp}(F)$ can then be strictly finer than $A(B)$. Definition 2.1 supplies left-totality; the proof should cite it.
- **The converse fails, and the paper never claims it, but the abstract's phrasing invites it.** Under (i-b) alone, $\neg$(ii-a) $\Rightarrow\neg$(i-a) always (a task whose $\operatorname{comp}$ is strictly finer than $\pi$ has disjoint, hence distinct, supports inside a block); but $\neg$(i-a) $\not\Rightarrow\neg$(ii-a) — a task may separate supports that still overlap. So (ii-a) is strictly *weaker* than (i-a) given (i-b), and the "two killing conditions" of the abstract are one condition and its consequence.
- **The empirical base is thinner than the tag suggests.** In the paper's own sweep, rigidity makes the antecedent of Theorem 3.2 true in exactly **16** cases — one per (configuration count, law) pair, every one of them the discrete boundary. "Zero counterexamples over the same exhaustive sweep" is true and nearly vacuous. I supplied the missing population: over all 687 laws at three configurations there are **1004** condition-(i) instances, **745 of them at proper boundaries**, with **0** (ii-a) failures. The theorem is now corroborated where it can actually bite; the paper should either say "over the 16 instances that sweep contains" or borrow a population like this one.

**Is condition (ii) honestly presented as occupancy-only content?** **Substantially yes** — Theorem 3.2's own aftermath, §5's closing paragraph, non-claim 6 and Deviation 1 all state it plainly, and Theorem 4.3 names the independent clause correctly. Three places overstate: the abstract's definition of condition (ii) carries no caveat; the abstract's "the two killing conditions are measured rather than assumed" counts one kill twice; and Definition 2.2 presents four clauses as coordinate when one of them is entailed by two others. The corrections are local (see *Sentences to rewrite*).

## THE TWO-SIDED GALOIS CONDITIONS AGAINST THE TERMINAL CYCLE B MACHINERY

Cycle B's poset convention is $M\sqsubseteq M'$ when $M'$ refines $M$ — coarser is lower — under which its Proposition 3.4, Proposition 3.6 and Theorem 3.7 are correctly antitone, and the L2 paper's §1.1 summary of them is faithful. **No error in the inherited machinery.**

But condition (i) is **not** the two-sided fixed point of that machinery, and the abstract's "Galois-minimality in both directions", §1.3's "two-sided fixed point" and the pin's same phrase are not earned:

- (i-b) uses $\operatorname{Pres}$ — Cycle B's right adjoint. Correct.
- (i-a) uses $\ker$, which is **not** $\operatorname{Core}$. $\operatorname{Core}(\mathfrak F)=\bigvee_F\operatorname{comp}(F)$ is monotone in the family; $\ker(\mathfrak F)=\bigwedge_F\ker(\operatorname{sup}_F)$ is antitone in it. They are adjoints of *different* connections ($\operatorname{Core}\dashv\operatorname{Pres}$; and $\ker$ paired with "constant on blocks"). Condition (i) welds the left adjoint of one to the right adjoint of the other.
- **Cycle B's own Theorem 3.8 is exactly this warning**: "the minimal-boundary core is not antitone… The Galois machinery of Theorem 3.7 therefore belongs to the availability relation, not to the minimal-boundary construction. This is a definitional consequence, and it is recorded because the two are easily conflated."

Measured consequence, which I regard as the single most illuminating number in this review: **the genuine two-sided Cycle B condition $\pi=\operatorname{Core}(\operatorname{Pres}_L(\pi))$ fixes *every* record — 2 of 2, 5 of 5, 15 of 15, 52 of 52 under DET, and 52 of 52 under the counter-law — while the mixed condition fixes exactly one at every count.** The axiom's selectivity is therefore neither inherited from Cycle B nor a discovery about competing minimalities: it is produced entirely by the substitution of $\ker$ for $\operatorname{Core}$, and the substitution overshoots from "everything is a fixed point" (Cycle B's own dead end, its Theorem 4.3) to "exactly one is". Rigidity is the signature of the mismatch. This does not falsify anything the paper proves; it relocates the explanation, and it means `BLOCKED-AT-CARRIER` names one of two available diagnoses — the other being *blocked at the choice of pairing*.
