## 1. Verdict and first decisive semantic counterexample

### 1.1 First counterexample — an accurate controller is not yet a clock

The pin never freezes, before model comparison, an operational predicate that
distinguishes a clock from an arbitrary finite autonomous controller with a
retained outcome.

Consider a finite autonomous two-colour controller. It has a complete
classical record, a fixed mechanism, finite resources, reciprocal disturbance,
separate constrained and autonomous descriptions, and a controlled influence
on the comparator. Its two derivations can agree on the complete transcript,
and a correct standard-quantum parent can predict independent held-out data.
The colour outcome can be calibrated and can drive a sequential or coherent
reader. Yet suppose no reader of that outcome estimates, orders, or tracks any
preregistered duration, phase, rate, recurrence window, or other timekeeping
task better than the no-clock baseline. This is a controller, not a clock.

Nothing in the frozen admission surface requires a model-independent clock
task, a clock-informativeness functional, or a minimum performance separation
from no-clock and mistuned controls. Terms such as `clock record`, `clock
intervention`, `resolution`, and `mistuned` already presuppose the
classification under test. T10 tests the accuracy of `P`, not specifically
the timekeeping content of the alleged record. T11 asks for a finite effect
that distinguishes controls, but an ordinary controller has finite effects.
T13 asks for sequential and coherent-reader tasks, but neither is necessarily
a timekeeping task. T14 can be made formally true by taking the named scalar
to have been a dummy input. Merely listing the dummy-parameter attack does not
give a positive admission criterion that rejects this packet.

Thus the current words can award a nominal L4 `operational clock` to a device
whose only earned property is held-out operational adequacy as a controller.
That is a semantic, not editorial, defect. It is repairable without choosing a
model or desired result, so `REVISE`, rather than `REJECT`, is the required
verdict.

### 1.2 Independent second defect — L6 admits vacuous scalar removal

Let the registered external scalar have at least two formal values but let the
reference predictor be independent of it. Then the complete predictor factors
through a scalar-independent quotient trivially. The root law, transcript,
held-out evidence, resources, and both routes may all be valid; nevertheless
no physically operative external input was replaced by a clock.

T14 and C42 correctly reject deletion of a parameter name, but the pin never
requires the pre-clock reference task to depend nontrivially on the scalar on
a separating reader family. Nor does Stage C explicitly freeze the scalar's
domain, operational role, reference law, nondegeneracy witness, and the
clock-record sufficiency map before prediction. Without those objects, the
L6 claim is not identified.

### 1.3 Independent third defect — L5 lacks two-clock adequacy

T16 requires separate carriers and interventions and excludes copying,
relabeling, and a common-controller alias. That is structural independence,
not timekeeping adequacy. A good clock A plus a separately embedded,
intervenable but time-insensitive device B can pass the printed T16 certificate.
No clause says that A and B must each separately pass the L4 clock task or that
their joint comparison must pass an independent held-out test. The label `TWO-
INDEPENDENT-CLOCKS` can therefore over-award a two-clock result.

## 8. Fresh semantic countermodels

These countermodels were generated independently and are not copied from the
pin.

### O1 — autonomous colour controller

The counterexample in Section 1 has records, resources, interactions, and
held-out adequacy but no timekeeping task. It separates `operational device`
from `operational clock`.

### O2 — vacuous scalar family

A reference family carries a formal scalar whose laws are constant over the
scalar domain. Factorization is exact but removes no physical dependence.

### O3 — prior marginalization masquerading as a clock

A predictor removes the scalar by averaging it against a fixed prior. It may
fit marginal data while failing every scalar-conditioned task. Information
was discarded, not supplied by an internal clock.

### O4 — one good clock plus one inert carrier

Clock A passes held-out timekeeping. Device B has its own carrier and
interventions but no timing information. Structural independence alone would
mislabel the pair as two clocks.

### O5 — two carriers with one physical bath

Two separately addressable carriers inherit phase and drift from a common
environmental oscillator. They are not copied and share no software
controller, yet their agreement is not independent evidence. A joint common-
cause audit is needed.

### O6 — common external calibration debt

Two clocks are calibrated against the same external laboratory time series
and then agree. The agreement tests stability relative to that standard; it
does not show that the standard became unnecessary.

### O7 — cyclic aliasing

A finite phase record assigns the same value to physically distinct numbers
of cycles. It is a valid local clock record inside a frozen window but cannot
support a global order or elapsed duration beyond that window.

### O8 — externally timed readout pulse

A device evolves autonomously, but its record exists only when an external
pulse opens the detector at a prescribed time. Its internal phase is not yet
an autonomous retained clock record unless the pulse, controller, and timing
resource are represented in `P`.

### O9 — dynamics visible outside the stopped task

All registered clock readers are constant on a sector, while an unregistered
energy-sensitive reader detects continued evolution. The sector is stopped
for the frozen task, not absolutely inert. This validates C40 and the late-
reader boundary.

### O10 — supplied-order reconstruction
